FlyRank ML Internship Capstone · Ranking Signal Analysis

From Search Signals to Human-Reviewed Content Priorities

A time-aware evaluation of observable search and engagement signals for content-level CTR estimation and transparent review prioritization.

1. Abstract

This study asks whether observable search and engagement signals can support reliable ranking of content-level CTR and help prioritize content for human review. Using FlyRank search intelligence data from January–March 2026, the analysis aggregates observations to the content-client level, trains an XGBoost regression model on January–February data, and evaluates it on March observations using a time-aware design. On the 118,091 March observations where a historical CTR baseline was available, the model achieved an MAE of 0.003543, RMSE of 0.008587, R² of 0.068647, and NDCG of 0.789890, while the historical CTR baseline achieved an MAE of 0.003413, RMSE of 0.016760, R² of -2.548167, and NDCG of 0.795028. The model therefore reduced large prediction errors and produced positive explanatory performance, but it did not improve ranking quality over the simple historical baseline, which also had higher mean per-client NDCG across 39 clients. The final output translates observed search signals into a transparent rule-based review queue, intended to support human investigation rather than automate content changes or make causal claims.

2. Introduction / Problem Statement

Search teams often have more content opportunities than they can manually investigate. A practical decision-support system therefore needs to identify which content observations deserve attention first while remaining transparent about what the available data can and cannot establish.

This capstone investigates two related questions:

  1. Can observable search and engagement signals help estimate observed content-level CTR?
  2. Can those signals be translated into a transparent ranking of content observations for human review?

Decision supported: Which content observations provide enough measurable signal to justify investigation first?

Data preparation

  • Daily observations were aggregated to the content-client-month level.
  • CTR was calculated as clicks divided by impressions.
  • Engagement rate was calculated from engaged sessions divided by sessions.
  • Average engagement seconds was calculated from total engagement seconds divided by sessions.
  • Observations with fewer than 10 impressions were excluded from the modeling and review queue.
  • Identifiers were used for grouping and evaluation, not as predictive model features.
  • Future trend fields were not used as predictors.

4. Methodology

4.1 Features

The model uses gsc_impressions, log_impressions, gsc_avg_position, engagement_rate, avg_engagement_sec, and a position bucket. Missing engagement-derived values were retained as missing rather than blindly converted to zero.

4.2 Model

An XGBoost regression model was trained with 200 estimators, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.05, subsampling 0.8, column subsampling 0.8, and random state 42.

4.3 Validation design

January and February were used for training and March was held out as a later evaluation period. This prevents March observations from entering model training, but it is important that March predictors are observed March signals. Therefore this is a time-aware later-period evaluation, not a pure pre-March forecasting experiment.

4.4 Historical baseline

The reference baseline uses historical CTR from January–February for content-client pairs that also appear in March. It covered 118,091 of 143,206 March observations (82.5%). Direct model-versus-baseline comparisons use this same common population.

4.5 Metrics and leakage checks

Performance was evaluated using MAE, RMSE, R², and NDCG. Clicks were not supplied as a feature, identifiers were excluded from prediction, and future trend variables were excluded. Impressions remain a target-proximal exposure variable because they appear in both the CTR denominator and the feature set; this is disclosed rather than treated as direct target leakage.

6. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The evaluation is time-aware but not a pure forecast because March features are contemporaneous observed signals.
  • Impressions are both an exposure feature and the denominator of CTR, making them target-proximal.
  • The historical baseline is available only for previously observed content-client pairs and covers 82.5% of the March test population.
  • The data does not include all potentially relevant factors, such as page semantics, search queries and intent, title/snippet details, technical SEO, backlinks, or complete seasonal context.
  • Feature importance is not causal evidence.
  • The analysis is observational and does not establish that changing a content attribute will improve future performance.
Supported conclusion: Observable search and engagement signals contain useful information for estimating and ranking observed CTR, but the tested XGBoost model did not demonstrate superior ranking performance to a simple historical-CTR baseline in the March 2026 evaluation.

This work does not establish Google's ranking algorithm, prove causal refresh effects, or guarantee individual content outcomes. It should not be used to automatically publish, rewrite, delete, redirect, or canonicalize content.

7. Ranked Recommendations

7.1 From signals to a human-review queue

The final queue is a transparent rule-based triage layer built from March 2026 observed signals. It is not a prediction of which changes will improve performance.

7.2 Priority logic

  • +2: CTR at or below the March 25th percentile.
  • +2: average position above 10 and at or below the March 75th percentile.
  • +1: impressions at or above the March median.

Because the March CTR 25th percentile was 0, the low-CTR condition primarily identifies observations with zero clicks despite having at least 10 impressions.

7.4 Human review playbook

  1. Inspect the actual page and its current content.
  2. Review the queries and search intent available to the authorized reviewer.
  3. Check title and SERP presentation.
  4. Review impressions, position, and CTR together rather than in isolation.
  5. Check recent content changes and possible seasonality.
  6. Verify tracking and data-quality issues.
  7. Check duplicates, canonicals, and related technical conditions before proposing changes.

No-go list: Do not automatically publish, rewrite, delete, redirect, or canonicalize content from this score alone. Do not treat priority as proof of causality or as a guaranteed future outcome.
